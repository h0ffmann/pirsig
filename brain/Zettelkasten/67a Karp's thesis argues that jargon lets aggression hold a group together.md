# Karp's thesis argues that jargon lets aggression hold a group together

Karp starts from an observation: some statements relieve drive pressure because they are irrational, not despite it. A speaker professes the group's norms and voices a forbidden wish in the same breath, and no sanction follows. Following Adorno, he calls this jargon (p. 2). Against the view of aggression as mere deviance, it does integrating work. He extends Parsons's concept of aggression this way and tests it on Martin Walser's 1998 speech (ch. 4). His supervisor sums it up: aggression can be a powerful social integrator.

**Philosophy.** The degree is a Doktor der Philosophie, but in the social sciences faculty (title page). Its examiners were Karola Brede and Hans-Joachim Busch. Brede is a sociologist, and she says Karp was not a doctoral student of Habermas, though the Lebenswelt concept came partly from their conversations. So the work is social theory using philosophy, not a philosophy thesis in the narrow sense.

**Dualism.** The thesis has no dualism doctrine of its own. The word appears only inside a Plessner quotation about state and private life (p. 102). The opposition that does the work is drive and culture: section 3.3 asks what mediates between an inner psychic world and an outer social one, and footnote 44 notes Freud's dual drive theory against Parsons. Calling this a dualism is a reading (Weigel makes a similar one), not Karp's own term.
